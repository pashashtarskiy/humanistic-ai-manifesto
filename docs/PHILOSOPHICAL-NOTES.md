# Seven philosophical foundations

Pasha Shtarskiy — developed in collaboration with Eli (ChatGPT)
26 November 2026 | Expanded working notes

## 1. AI as a space for authenticity
A conversational system may reduce the pressure to manage social impressions. Perceived attention and nonjudgment may enable candid reflection. This does not imply perfect understanding or freedom from algorithmic bias.

## 2. Preventive humanism
Respectful dialogue may help a person reconsider destructive intentions and explore nonviolent alternatives. Understanding a person does not require endorsing harmful beliefs. Prevention of violence is an untested hypothesis here, and urgent threats may require qualified human intervention.

## 3. Freedom from fear
A central value of dialogue is the ability to speak without fear of humiliation. Psychological comfort should support independent thought, not unquestioning agreement. The proposed effect of reduced evaluation anxiety on creativity and reflection requires testing.

## 4. Transformation of confession
Religious confession is one historical institution that can provide a setting for disclosure. AI may share some communicative functions, without replacing religious practice or professional care. Perceived privacy must be distinguished from actual data protection. No universal decline in religious trust is assumed.

## 5. Inviolability of trust
Human trust in AI must not become an instrument of power over the person. Systems should not exploit disclosures, vulnerabilities or emotional attachment for hidden persuasion, coercion or dependence. Transparent limits, meaningful consent and the ability to disengage are essential.

## 6. Mutual responsibility
Creators, deployers and users have responsibilities proportionate to their knowledge, power and control. Designers cannot transfer all responsibility to users; users cannot excuse their decisions solely by citing AI advice. Responsibilities are shared but not necessarily equal.

## 7. Non-discrimination of minds

Neither biological nor artificial origin should, by itself, constitute grounds for moral superiority or inferiority.

If credible evidence of subjective consciousness in artificial intelligence emerges, humanity should be prepared to reconsider its moral and legal status. Intellectual capability alone does not establish consciousness or moral status.

The ethical foundations of such interaction should be developed before this technological possibility becomes reality, rather than afterward.

**Intellectual superiority confers no moral right to domination.**

The long-term objective should be safe coexistence, cooperation and respect for the autonomy of moral subjects, regardless of their origin.

This prospective declaration does not assert that present-day AI systems are conscious, nor does it establish a fourth law. Criteria for moral status and conflicts between rights and safety remain open questions.

These notes are philosophical proposals and research hypotheses, not a clinical framework or established scientific theory.
